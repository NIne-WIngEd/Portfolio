The question after the A

The experiment ended before the question did.

My teacher gave me an A. I had a question.

In seventh grade, I brought a partly moldy piece of bread to science class. Following the instructions, I named what I thought I could identify and wrote down the shapes. The exercise was finished, and my teacher was pleased.

But I couldn’t stop wondering: had I found everything, or only the things the instructions had taught me to look for?

I tried again with a microscope, a microbiology book and my teacher’s help. The bread went bad before I could finish the investigation. I never got the complete answer I wanted.

Hey, I tried, though.

When I wrote about this years later, that was the part I wanted to keep: the attempt, and the question that made me attempt it. A grade could finish the assignment. It hadn’t finished my curiosity.

Learning in pieces

My learning method is a mosaic.

During the unexpected pause of COVID, I tried to find the common root of my interests. I started with computational biology, moved toward quantum chemistry, spent time with particle physics, and found geology and earth science appealing too. Software and automation kept appearing along the way.

The interests kept changing. Research and creation were the thread I could recognize between them.

When I approach a subject, I tend to break it into smaller questions and spend time with each. Then I try to see the larger picture they make together. I like practical classes for the same reason: a theory becomes more interesting when I can see how it interacts with something around me.

Sometimes that something is a cell. Sometimes it’s my mother putting hot chocolate in the freezer and leaving me with another question.

I expect my interests to keep evolving. I want to understand enough to notice a connection, ask a better question, or make something with it.

A conversation at sea

My interest in systems began with listening.

Before I started high school, I joined my father on a fifteen-day cruise.

He’s a sailor. We talked about our family, our lives and the future I was already worrying about. Those conversations also reached the systems he worked with: battery efficiency, automation, losses that kept recurring, and things he wished he could change.

I started looking into them. Engineering became easier to imagine when it was attached to a problem someone I loved actually encountered.

That conversation is part of how I explain my interest in systems. Physics, robotics, CAD, simulations and research gave me ways to pursue it. The interest began with listening.

I value the time I have with my father. When I wrote about the future I wanted, being near family mattered alongside the engineering program I hoped to join.

An idea the neighbors could use

The useful part of a competition model found another home.

The building’s reservoir would overflow if someone didn’t stop the motor in time.

Our community was sixty-four families. We met each month to talk about problems around us and how to make living there better. Water management was one of those problems: the motor was controlled manually, and a missed shutoff wasted water and electricity.

I had already built a smart-city model for a robotics competition. One feature controlled a motor using a signal from the reservoir’s water level. I hadn’t made it expecting that a piece of the competition model would end up in a practical setting.

But the connection was there. I proposed adapting that feature to the building’s reservoir. After the community approved it, I implemented it. The automatic control solved the overflow problem.

When I wrote about it, I talked about three things. My learning had become useful. People had helped bring an idea to life. And the experience had made change feel more possible to the community.

I remember the gold medal from the competition, too. I got to bite it. But the reservoir gave that work another place to matter.

It depends on the circumstance

A playful question turned into a discussion about values.

We were talking about Hogwarts houses. Everyone chose a house. Nobody chose Slytherin.

The reasons were familiar: ambition, calculation, pride, being shrewd. I asked whether those qualities were necessarily bad.

What had begun as a playful question became a conversation about values. Staying calm and looking for a benefit could be useful. Being decisive could help someone carry out a plan. Even protecting someone could become confining through another person’s perspective.

I wanted to understand the circumstance in which a quality was being used.

That was the insight I kept from the conversation: context changes how a quality looks. Before choosing a judgment, I want to understand the situation.
